# Before I knew You
Dear Samee,

I have started writing this a number of times in my head.

Somewhere between doing absolutely nothing and doing a hundred other things that needed my attention, I would find myself thinking about how I would begin all this. Sometimes I had entire paragraphs figured out. Sometimes it was just a sentence that I thought I should remember before it disappeared. And sometimes, I would simply think about you and leave it at that, because not every thought needs to be turned into words immediately.

But the problem with writing about someone who has occupied so much of your mind is that you never really know where to begin.

Do you begin with the first time you noticed them? The first conversation that stayed with you a little longer than it should have? The first time you realised that their happiness mattered to you in a way you hadn't quite prepared yourself for? Or do you begin much later, when you have already accumulated enough memories to look back and realise that something which once seemed ordinary had quietly become a part of your life?

I don't know.

Maybe that is why I am beginning here.

Not with the day we met, not with a particular memory and not with some grand explanation of how you became important to me. I don't want to pretend that I understood everything while it was happening. I didn't. There were things I understood only much later, things I probably still don't understand completely and things that I might have understood differently had I been a little more patient with myself and with you.

I have never been particularly good at leaving a thought alone once it has found its way into my head. I take it apart, look at it from different directions, try to understand where it came from and what it means. Sometimes this helps. Sometimes I end up standing in the middle of a perfectly ordinary feeling, looking for an explanation that was never really required.

And somewhere along the way, you became one of those thoughts.

Not because everything about you was complicated. You weren't some problem I had to solve and I hope you know that. It was more that knowing you made me realise how little we actually understand about the people we think we know. We see a smile, hear a story, notice a habit, remember a sentence. We put all these little things together and call it knowing someone.

But a person is never just the things we have managed to notice about them.

There is always a part of them that exists outside our understanding. A part they may not know how to explain themselves. A part that comes out only when they feel safe enough or when life has left them with no energy to pretend that everything is fine. And then there are parts that we may never get to see at all.

I think I have spent a fair amount of time trying to understand the person behind the things I could see.

And perhaps, at times, I forgot that understanding someone is not the same as having an answer for everything they do.

But we will get to that.

For now, I want to talk about you.

The difficult thing about writing about you is that I don't want to write a character. I don't want to take all the things I love about you, arrange them into neat little paragraphs and give the world a version of you that is so perfect to exist in real life. That would be easy, perhaps. It would also be unfair to you.

You are a person, Samee. Not an idea. Not a collection of memories that I can revisit whenever I want to feel something. Not the sum of the things you have done for me, the things you have said or even the things that hurt.

You are much more than what I have experienced of you.

And I am writing this knowing that whatever I put down on these pages will still be incomplete. I can write a hundred and fifty pages or three hundred or keep writing until I have exhausted every memory I can recall and I will still have only my side of the story. There will be moments I remember differently from you. There will be things I misunderstood. There will be versions of certain days that exist only in my head because that is how I experienced them.

I cannot write your entire truth.

But I can write mine.

I can write about the girl I came to know, the person I saw beneath the things she chose to show me and the little details that stayed with me long after the conversations themselves had ended. I can write about the things that made me laugh, the things that made me worry, the things I admired and the things I struggled to understand. I can write about the ways in which I was right about you, the ways in which I was probably wrong and the times when my own fears made it difficult to tell the difference.

And I can try to be honest about myself while doing it.

Because it would be convenient to write this as though I was always the person who knew what to say, always the person who understood, always the person whose intentions were good enough to make everything okay.

But that would be another kind of dishonesty.

I have cared about you. I have also, at times, struggled with the ways in which I cared. There is a difference between wanting someone to be okay and knowing how to make them feel okay. A difference between wanting to protect someone and understanding when your concern begins to feel like pressure. A difference between having something to say and knowing whether it is the right time to say it.

I have had to learn some of these differences the hard way.

There have been moments when I wished I could go back and handle things a little differently. Not necessarily change everything that happened, because I don't know what our lives would have looked like if every conversation had gone perfectly. Perhaps some things would have been easier. Perhaps we would simply have found different things to struggle with.

But there are words I would choose differently. There are silences I would understand better. There are moments when I wish I had listened without immediately trying to explain, fix or make sense of what I was hearing.

I am not writing this to reopen those moments. I am writing it because they are a part of the story too and because I don't want to remember only the parts of us that are easy to remember.

You deserve to be seen as a whole person, not through a version of events in which I am always right.

And perhaps I deserve that honesty too.

You see, I have always found it interesting how certain people enter our lives and become connected to parts of us we didn't even realise were waiting to be understood. Not necessarily because they do something extraordinary. Sometimes it is simply the way they speak about something, the way they react to a situation or the way an ordinary conversation leaves you thinking about something long after it has ended.

It is a little like music, I suppose.

You can hear a song a hundred times and know exactly when the next note is going to arrive. And yet, on a particular day, for reasons you may not be able to explain, one line lands differently. Nothing about the recording has changed. The song is the same. But you are not quite the same person who heard it yesterday.

I think people can be like that too.

Sometimes, without intending to, they bring your attention to something that was already there inside you. They make you notice a fear you had been avoiding, a tenderness you had kept to yourself or a part of your personality that you had never really stopped to examine. And you cannot always tell whether they have changed you or whether they have simply helped you notice a change that was already taking place.

I don't know exactly where you fit into that explanation.

Maybe somewhere between the two.

What I do know is that when I look back at the time I have known you, I don't see only a sequence of conversations or a list of things that happened. I see a period of my life in which I learned things about another person, certainly, but also about myself. About attachment. About expectations. About how easily care can become tangled with fear. About the fact that two people can genuinely matter to each other and still fail to understand what the other person needs in a particular moment.

And about how much there is to learn when we stop treating our own understanding of a situation as the only possible truth.

I am still learning.

I suspect that is something I should admit at the beginning of this rather than wait until the final chapter to make myself sound wiser than I am.

There is also something else I want you to know.

This is not an argument. It is not a collection of evidence for why I felt the way I felt and it is definitely not an attempt to convince you of anything. You don't have to agree with every word here for it to be true to my experience. You don't have to remember every moment in the same way I do. And you certainly don't have to find yourself in every description.

I just wanted, for once, to give these thoughts somewhere to exist outside my head.

Perhaps that is what writing has always been for me. A way of taking something that feels too large or too tangled to hold comfortably and giving it a shape. Sometimes that shape is a song. Sometimes it is a journal entry. Sometimes it is a page filled with thoughts that seem unrelated until I read them again after a few days and realise that they were all trying to reach the same place.

This time, it is all about you. A journal, Samee journal.

Nope a letter perhaps. Just a very long one.

And the title seemed obvious once I stopped trying to come up with something clever.

A Letter to Samee.

Because, at the end of all these thoughts, all these memories, all the things I could say about the person you are and the person I have known, I am still just one person trying to tell another person what she has meant to him.

Her Through My Eyes.

That part matters just as much.

Because these pages will be about you, but they will also reveal the person who is looking at you. The things I notice. The things I remember. The things I find beautiful. The things I don't understand. The things I have learnt to question about my own perspective.

Perhaps that is the strange thing about writing about someone you care about. You begin thinking you are trying to describe them and somewhere along the way you realise you have been describing yourself too.

So, before I tell you about the things I remember, I should probably tell you one last thing.

I don't think I knew, when you first became a part of my life, that one day I would sit down and try to put all of this into words. I don't think either of us could have known what the months ahead would bring, which memories would stay, which conversations would matter or which seemingly insignificant moments would become important only in retrospect.

Life rarely announces the things we will remember.

Most of the time, we are simply living it.

We speak without knowing which sentence someone might carry with them. We laugh without knowing that years later, we might remember the exact feeling of that moment. We worry, misunderstand, make up [or out ;-) ], grow, change our minds and keep moving. And then one day we look back and find that a person has become part of a period of our lives in ways we could never have planned.

You became a part of mine, Samee.

And whatever the future does with that fact, it remains a fact about my past and the person I am today.

I don't know if a journal or a letter can ever adequately explain that. I don't know if these pages will manage to hold everything I want them to hold. There will probably be days when I write too much, days when I cannot find the right words and days when I will have to remind myself that not every feeling needs a metaphor to make it meaningful.

But I want to try.

Not because I believe every important thing must be preserved forever. Not because I think a person can be captured in honest journal entries,  like businesses. And not because I have some perfect conclusion waiting at the end of all this.

Simply because, for a period of my life, knowing you mattered enough for me to want to remember it properly.

And I think that is a good enough place to begin.

So, this is where I will leave the first part.

Before the memories, before the stories, before I try to tell you what I saw in you and how I came to see it.

Just this.

There was a time when I didn't know you.

And then, somewhere along the way, I did.

I suppose the rest of this is my attempt to tell you what happened in between.

> Yours,
> D


# The First Time I Noticed You
February 2024.

RBI internship interview.

Funny how some memories stay with you without really having a reason to. You don't necessarily remember the entire day, the people you met or even the things you were thinking about at the time. But there are certain little moments that somehow find a place in your memory and decide to stay there.

I remember you coming out of your interview and talking to the other candidates about how it went. The panel had asked you about CBDC and you were telling everyone about your experience.

You seemed quite happy, quite confident and very sure of what you were saying. There was an ease in the way you spoke, the way you put your thoughts together. You didn't seem to be struggling to find the right words or wondering whether you should say something. You just spoke.

I think that was the first time I noticed you.

Not in the way people usually mean when they say they noticed someone. There wasn't some extraordinary moment and I certainly didn't walk away thinking I had just met someone who would eventually find her way into so many of my thoughts. You were simply another candidate who had appeared for an interview and was now sharing her experience with the rest of us.

Perhaps it was the confidence. Perhaps it was the way you spoke. Or perhaps it was just one of those things that you don't think much about at the time, only to realise later that the memory has somehow stayed with you.

I don't know.

And I suppose I don't need to.
***

A few days later, it was the first day of our internship.

I was standing outside the lobby, waiting, when you walked in.

Your face looked familiar.

I remember trying to place you, trying to figure out where I had seen you before. And then, for some reason, I did something I don't think I had ever done before.

I started a conversation with a complete stranger.

"Intern?"

That was my opening line.

Quite a remarkable beginning, if you ask me.

I could have said something else, perhaps. Introduced myself, asked your name or come up with something a little more interesting. But apparently, that was all my brain could manage at the time.

You were an intern, of course.

And that was that. At least, that was how it seemed then.

I didn't think much about the conversation afterwards. There wasn't much to think about, really. Two people who were going to spend the next few weeks in the same place had started talking. It was ordinary enough.

But now, when I look back, I find it a little amusing that I remember that one word so clearly.

Intern?

I wonder how many conversations begin with something equally insignificant. How many people walk into our lives through the most ordinary of circumstances, without either person having the faintest idea of what lies ahead.

We don't really pay attention to beginnings while we are living them. We are too busy with the day itself, with whatever is happening around us, with the thousand other things occupying our minds.

It is only later that we start looking back.

And by then, even a question asked outside a lobby can become a memory worth writing about.
***

16 July 2024.

I was returning after finishing the internship.

You came to the railway station to see me off.

We sat on a bench on the platform. I don't remember us talking much that day. Perhaps we had things to say and couldn't quite find the words or perhaps there wasn't really anything that needed to be said.

Some silences are uncomfortable. You keep looking for something to fill them with, some conversation to keep things moving. And then there are silences that don't ask you to do anything at all.

That morning was a little like the latter.

I remember the small things. Things that probably wouldn't mean much to anyone else and which I might have forgotten had I not somehow held on to that particular day.

A brief touch. A little hesitation. And then, after a while, neither of us seemed particularly interested in moving away from it.

I don't know what was going through your mind. I can only tell you that, for once, mine seemed to have found something better to do than keep running.

You know how it is with me. There is almost always something going on in my head. One thought leads to another and before I know it, I am thinking about things that have absolutely nothing to do with what is happening in front of me.

But sitting there, beside you, it all seemed to quieten down for a little while.

Not because anything had been explained. Not because I had suddenly figured something out. There was no particular thought I could point to and say that was what changed things.

It was just a different kind of quiet.

The platform carried on with its usual business. People came and went, trains arrived and departed and time did what time always does. We were still sitting on that same bench, in that same place, with the same world moving around us.

Only, for a few minutes, I wasn't paying much attention to any of it.

I think that was the first time I noticed something about you that had very little to do with the way you spoke or how confidently you put your thoughts across.

There was another side to you. A quieter one, perhaps. One that didn't need to explain itself.

Or maybe I had simply never sat still long enough to notice it.

I have wondered since whether that day felt different to you too. Perhaps you remember it just as clearly, perhaps you remember only bits of it or perhaps some other detail stayed with you that I haven't even thought about.

I won't try to decide that for you.

I just know that I remember the journey back a little differently from how I might have remembered it otherwise.

And I suppose that says enough.

When I think about those three moments now, I sometimes find it strange how little I understood at the time.

In February, you were the girl who had come out of an interview and was confidently telling everyone how it had gone.

Then you were the familiar face outside the lobby, the person I somehow managed to start a conversation with using a single word.

And then there was July.

I don't think I could have explained what was different about that morning, even if someone had asked me. Some things take time to settle in your mind. You carry them around for a while before you realise that they have stayed with you longer than you expected.

Perhaps that is why I like looking back at the beginning.

There is no need to make it into something it wasn't. No need to pretend that I knew where things were going or that every little moment carried some hidden meaning waiting to be discovered.

I didn't know.

I was just there, living those days as they came.

And you were there too.

It would be a little unfair to say that I knew you from the first day. I knew a few things about you, collected a few impressions and gradually began to recognise the person behind them. But knowing someone is a rather long process and I was only at the beginning of it.

There was still so much I hadn't seen. So many things I didn't know about you and so many things I hadn't yet understood about myself.

For now, there was a girl I had noticed after an interview, a conversation that began outside a lobby and a morning at a railway station that I have never quite managed to forget.

Nothing more needs to be said about it just yet.

Some memories are better left with a little room around them.

And perhaps, if I had known then that I would one day be writing about all this, I might have paid a little more attention.

Or perhaps not.

Maybe the best part is that I didn't know.

I just happened to notice you.

And then, little by little, I kept noticing.

# The Half Hours
I think there is a certain point in knowing someone when you stop needing a reason to meet them.

Initially, there is always something to talk about. You ask questions, learn about each other, try to figure out what kind of a person you are dealing with. There are stories to exchange, things to discover and a hundred little details that are new to you.

And then, at some point, the conversations become a little less about getting to know someone and a little more about simply being around them.

I don't know when that happened with us.

Perhaps there wasn't a particular day when things changed. Maybe it happened so gradually that neither of us noticed. One day we were meeting, and then we were meeting again, and eventually it became a part of the day.

Our everyday post-office Gandhi Maidan meets.

Half an hour. Sometimes forty-five minutes.

And honestly, what did we even do there?

Nothing, really.

We would sit there, share whatever random thoughts had crossed our minds, talk about how the day had gone, listen to each other's experiences and somehow manage to spend a good part of an hour without doing anything that would sound particularly interesting if someone asked us about it.

No elaborate plans. No special occasion. No particular agenda that needed to be fulfilled before we could leave.

Just two people sitting in an open playground, talking about things.

Sometimes, I wonder whether we realise the value of such moments while we are living them. We are so used to measuring our days by what we accomplish, where we go, what we learn and how much of our time we have managed to use productively. Even our leisure time somehow comes with an expectation that it should be spent doing something.

But those meetings were different.

There wasn't much to achieve there.

I would come with whatever had happened during my day and you would have your own set of stories. Some things were worth discussing, some were probably not worth discussing at all and some were just thoughts that needed to be said out loud. We would share them anyway.

And then we would sit there a little longer.

I don't remember every conversation we had. I wish I did, sometimes. It would be nice to go back and read through those days, to know what we talked about on a particular afternoon, which random thought made us laugh or what you had told me about your day that I might have forgotten.

But perhaps that is the strange thing about everyday memories.

You don't store them properly.

You don't think of taking a mental photograph every time something ordinary happens. You don't tell yourself to remember the way a conversation unfolded or how the afternoon felt. You just live through it, assuming that there will be another day, another conversation, another half an hour when you will sit together and talk about whatever has happened since the last time you met.

And most of the time, you are right.

There is another day.

Until one day, you look back and realise that all those ordinary days have quietly become something you miss.

I think we often underestimate how much of a person we come to know through the things that don't seem important enough to remember.

It is easy to remember the big conversations. The ones where someone tells you something significant about their life, where you learn something you hadn't known before or where a single sentence makes you reconsider something.

But what about all the other conversations?

The ones about absolutely nothing. The stories that begin somewhere and end somewhere else. The random opinions, the unnecessary details, the things that happened during the day that probably wouldn't interest anyone else quite as much.

You learn a person there too.

Perhaps even more than you realise.

You begin to understand the way they think, the things they notice, what makes them laugh, what irritates them and the little ways in which they look at the world. You start recognising their patterns without consciously trying to. You become familiar with the kinds of things they might say, the experiences they are likely to share and the details they consider worth telling you.

And without really planning for it, you make room for their everyday life in yours.

I think that was what those Gandhi Maidan meetings gave me.

Not some extraordinary revelation about you, but the chance to know you in small portions, over and over again.

There was no need for either of us to be particularly interesting all the time. We could talk about the day, share a thought, listen and let the conversation go wherever it wanted to.

And I liked that.

I liked that we didn't need to do something special to meet. That a perfectly ordinary day could be reason enough to spend some time together. That even after the day's work was done, there was still this little window of time when we could sit down and exchange whatever was on our minds.

Thirty minutes. Forty-five, perhaps.

It doesn't sound like much when you put it that way.

But then, time has always been a rather strange thing to measure.

Some days pass in front of you without leaving much behind. Other days are made up of small, unremarkable moments that you don't really understand the value of until much later. And sometimes, the same half an hour can feel entirely different depending on who you spend it with.

I suppose I was beginning to understand that.

There is something else about those meetings that I find myself thinking about.

We weren't always doing something. We weren't always going somewhere. We didn't need to turn every meeting into an event that would make a good story later.

We could just sit there.

And talk or probably spend time in complete silence, silence that is not uncomfortable.

I know how simple that sounds. Perhaps that is why I keep coming back to it. There is nothing particularly poetic about two people sitting in a playground after work, sharing the day's experiences. People do it every day, in different cities, in different playgrounds, over cups of tea, on benches, outside offices and on their way home.

It is one of the most ordinary things in the world.

But ordinary things are not necessarily insignificant.

Sometimes, the best part of your day is not something that happened during the day itself, but the opportunity to tell someone about it afterwards. Something goes wrong, something funny happens, you have a thought you cannot quite get out of your head and somewhere in the back of your mind, there is the knowledge that you will get to share it with someone.

You don't necessarily think about it in those terms. You simply look forward to meeting them.

And I don't know exactly when our meetings became something I looked forward to.

I only know that they became a part of those days.

Gandhi Maidan was where we would sit for a while after the office, with no real plan for what we were going to talk about. Whatever had happened that day would find its way into the conversation. Whatever was on our minds would somehow become part of that half an hour.

And then we would leave.

The day would continue. There would be other things to do, other places to be, other thoughts to occupy us.

Until the next time.

I think that is what I like most about remembering it now. There was no grand announcement that we had become familiar with each other. No point at which either of us had to acknowledge that we were becoming a part of each other's routine.

We simply kept showing up.

And sometimes, that is how familiarity grows. Not through one long conversation that tells you everything about a person, but through many small ones that leave you knowing a little more than you did before.

A little more about their day. A little more about their thoughts. A little more about the person they are when there isn't anything particularly important happening.

You don't notice the accumulation immediately.

You just keep talking.

I don't know whether you ever thought much about those half hours. Maybe they were just a part of your routine too. Maybe you remember them differently. I won't try to give your memories a meaning they may not have had for you.

But from where I stand, looking back at that time, those meetings were something I am glad we had.

They were ordinary.

They were ours, in the small way that a shared routine can belong to two people.

And for a while, they were simply a part of life.

I think, at the time, I was too busy living those days to realise that one day I might miss something as simple as sitting beside you, listening to whatever you had to say about yours.

Funny, isn't it?

We spend so much time waiting for something memorable to happen that we sometimes forget we are already making memories.

And perhaps those half hours were exactly that.

Not something I knew I would remember.

Just something I got to live with you.

# Somewhere Between a Hi and Good Night
Dear Samee,

I was going through our old chats the other day. It's strange how a conversation can look so ordinary when you're having it, and then, a couple of years later, you find yourself sitting there reading it again.

A hi.

A busy ho?

A question about some work that wasn't getting done, a complaint about the people around us, a random picture, a joke that probably wasn't even that funny. Sometimes a conversation would last for a few minutes, sometimes it would stretch much longer than either of us had probably planned.

And then there were the conversations that didn't really have a beginning or an end. One thing would lead to another, we'd get distracted, return to something we'd forgotten to discuss and somehow find another reason to keep talking.

Nothing particularly extraordinary, if you ask me.

At least, not at the time.

I think that's what makes these chats a little difficult to explain now. There isn't one particular conversation I can point towards and say, See, this was the moment. There wasn't a grand announcement, no sudden realisation that things had changed. If anything, it was a collection of small things that probably didn't look like much individually.

You asking whether I was in the library (during our RBI stint).

Me asking if you'd had lunch.

One of us complaining about work and the other joining in, because apparently suffering becomes a little more manageable when you have someone to complain with.

And, of course, the occasional unsolicited advice, which both of us were equally qualified to give and equally likely to ignore.

You had your research report to worry about, your exams, your endless questions about what the people at the office wanted from you. I had my own share of confusion, deadlines and the general business of pretending that I knew what I was doing.

We helped each other where we could. Sometimes it was something useful, like sharing a thought or explaining a concept. Sometimes it was just telling the other person not to take so much pressure.

I remember you worrying about whether you'd be able to manage your work and me telling you that you'd figure it out. I don't know how convincing I sounded, considering I was often trying to convince myself of the same thing.

But I meant it.

There was something nice about being able to discuss these things without having to make them sound more important than they were. You could tell me that something was going badly and we could talk about it. We could disagree, make fun of each other and then return to the original problem as if we hadn't just taken a rather unnecessary detour.

Perhaps that was one of the things I liked about talking to you. The conversations didn't always have to be meaningful to be worth having.

Sometimes, they could just be conversations.

And you know, I think we were quite good at that.
***

We had our own little way of talking, too.

There was always some unnecessary teasing, some ridiculous argument that neither of us was particularly interested in winning and the occasional message that made absolutely no sense without the preceding twenty messages. There were jokes that probably wouldn't survive being explained to anybody else. There were complaints followed by laughter, serious discussions interrupted by something stupid and moments when one of us would say something just to see how the other would react.

You'd tell me to change my display picture. I'd ask why. You'd find something wrong with the next one too.

I would give you advice and you'd question my qualifications to give it. Fair enough, honestly.

And somewhere in between all this, we'd talk about the actual things going on in our lives.

I don't think either of us made a conscious effort to build this familiarity. It just happened while we were busy doing other things. We were trying to finish work, get through exams, figure out what came next and deal with whatever the day had brought with it. Talking to each other became one more thing that happened along the way.

Only, unlike most of those other things, it didn't always feel like something that needed to be completed.

Sometimes a conversation would end because one of us had to leave. Sometimes because it was getting late. Sometimes we'd simply run out of things to say, at least for the moment.

And then, after a while, one of us would return with another hi.

I don't know if you noticed how natural that had become. I certainly didn't stop to think about it. There was always another thing to tell you, another question to ask or something I had seen that I thought you'd find amusing.

Or perhaps I just wanted to know what you were doing.

The distinction wasn't particularly clear to me then.
***

Looking back, I realise that a lot of our conversations were made up of things that would be difficult to put into words if I were trying to make the story sound impressive.

The library. Coffee. Research papers. Exams. Interviews. Random complaints about work. The occasional discussion that began with a practical question and somehow ended up somewhere neither of us had expected.

There were days when you were worried about something and I'd try to help. Days when I was frustrated and you would listen. Days when one of us had very little to say, but the other would still ask how things were going.

And then there were the little things.

A message wishing me/you luck. A reminder not to take too much pressure. A question about whether I'd/ you'd have reached safely. The simple instruction to rest when the day had been long.

Nothing that would make sense to somebody reading our chat without knowing us.

But I suppose that is the problem with small things. They rarely announce themselves as important. They don't arrive with a label, asking you to remember them. They just become part of the day and you get used to having them there.

I think we underestimate how much of knowing someone happens like this.

Not through the stories we carefully choose to tell, but through the things we mention without thinking. The complaints we repeat. The worries we share before we've figured out how to explain them properly. The random details that would probably be forgotten by anyone else, but which somehow become familiar to the person on the other side of the conversation.

You begin to learn how someone thinks, what makes them laugh, what they worry about and when they are trying to pretend that everything is fine.

Or at least, you begin to think you know.

And perhaps that's another thing I have learnt since then: knowing someone is not the same as knowing everything about them. There will always be parts of another person that remain theirs alone. But you can still come to know the little things. You can pay attention. You can try to understand.

I hope I did some of that, even if I didn't always get it right.
***

There is one thing I find particularly interesting about our old chats.

When I read them now, I can see how much of the ordinary day we used to share with each other. Not necessarily the important parts. Just the day itself.

# The Things You Never Sat Down to Teach Me
